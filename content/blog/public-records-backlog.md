Public sector backlogs — records requests, permit applications, benefit claims, case files, licence renewals — share a structure. Inbound volume exceeds processing capacity, the deficit accumulates, statutory deadlines are missed, and the effort spent on backlog-related enquiries reduces the capacity available to clear it.

Automation is frequently proposed. It frequently disappoints, because it is applied to the visible step rather than the constraining one.

## Find the actual constraint first

Before automating anything, measure where elapsed time goes on a sample of cases. The answer is rarely what the team assumes.

In most backlogged processes, the largest components are:

- **Waiting for a missing piece of information**, usually discovered late.
- **Locating relevant material** across systems and files.
- **Rework** caused by incomplete or incorrect intake.
- **Handoffs** between people and teams.
- **Status enquiries** consuming the time of the people processing the work.

Actual determination time — the part requiring judgement — is often a small fraction. Automating it would help least, and is the hardest and riskiest to automate.

This measurement takes days and changes what gets built. Skipping it is why so many public sector automation projects deliver a working system that does not reduce the backlog.

## Where automation reliably helps

**Intake completeness checking.** The highest-return intervention in almost every backlogged process. Checking a submission for completeness on arrival and requesting what is missing immediately compresses the largest single component of elapsed time. It requires no decision automation, carries essentially no risk, and improves the applicant's experience as well as the queue.

**Classification and routing.** Getting each item to the right team, first time, with the right priority. Misrouting is a common and invisible source of delay, because the clock keeps running while an item sits in the wrong queue.

**Deadline tracking.** Many processes carry statutory response times. A system that knows every item's deadline and surfaces the queue in deadline order rather than arrival order prevents the most consequential failures.

**Locating material.** For any process requiring review of a file or historical records, indexed retrieval with citation removes a large amount of searching. Covered in more depth in our post on record retrieval.

**Redaction assistance.** For records disclosure, identifying candidate material for redaction and presenting it to a reviewer is a large time saving on what is otherwise painstaking manual work. Covered further in our post on redaction pipelines — with the important caveat that automated redaction requires human verification, because an unredacted disclosure is not recoverable.

**Status transparency.** A significant share of staff time in backlogged processes goes to answering "where is my application". Automated status visibility removes that load and returns capacity directly to processing.

That last one is worth noting because it does not look like document automation at all, and it frequently produces the largest immediate capacity gain.

## Where to be careful

**Determinations affecting rights or entitlements.** Automating the decision to grant or refuse is a different category of risk, subject to legal constraints in many jurisdictions around automated decision-making, and requires explainability, appeal rights and human oversight. The safer and usually more valuable pattern is to automate the assembly of the file and the mechanical checks, leaving the determination to a person.

**Anything the applicant cannot contest.** If an automated step affects an outcome, the applicant needs to be able to understand and challenge it. Systems whose behaviour cannot be explained do not meet that standard.

**Redaction, without verification.** The asymmetry is total: an over-redacted disclosure is corrected on appeal; an under-redacted one cannot be undone.

**Historical backlogs with degraded material.** Old cases may involve poor-quality documents, missing files and superseded formats. Do not assume the pipeline built for current intake will handle the archive.

## Sequencing that works

**Start with intake.** Completeness checking and routing on new submissions stops the backlog growing. A backlog with an unfixed intake process is a bucket being bailed with the tap running.

**Then deadline visibility.** Reorder the existing queue by risk and deadline. This changes outcomes before any processing improvement lands.

**Then retrieval and assembly.** Reduce the time per case for the work that remains.

**Then targeted mechanical checks.** Automate the rule-based portions of the determination where the rules are genuinely mechanical, leaving judgement to people.

**Measure throughput at every stage.** Backlog reduction is a function of capacity minus inflow, and interventions that improve one while degrading the other are common. A completeness check that adds an intake step and reduces submission quality has made things worse.

## The transparency requirement

Public sector deployments carry expectations that commercial ones often do not: the public may be entitled to know that automation is used, how it works, and what recourse exists.

Building for that from the start is much easier than retrofitting it. In practice it means: documented purpose and scope, published in accessible language; a clear statement of what is automated and what is not; provenance for every automated output; human review for anything affecting an outcome; a route to challenge; and an audit trail sufficient to reconstruct any individual case.

These requirements are usually described as a burden. They also happen to describe a well-engineered system, which is a useful coincidence when making the case internally.

## A realistic ambition

A backlogged process with an intake completeness check, deadline-ordered queues, indexed retrieval and automated status visibility will clear work substantially faster with the same staff — not because determinations got faster, but because the waiting, searching, rework and enquiry-handling around them shrank.

That is a sufficient outcome, it is achievable, and it does not require asking a regulator or a citizen to accept a machine deciding their case.
